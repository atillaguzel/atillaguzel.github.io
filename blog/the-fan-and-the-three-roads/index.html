The fan and the three roads

25 November 2026

It is the last day of April, and somebody is at the kitchen table deciding whether to fix their mortgage for two years or for five. The Bank of England published its report that morning. They open it to see where the Bank thinks prices are heading, and look for the one line that will tell them.

There is no line. There are three, lettered A, B and C, and the report takes care to say that none of them is the forecast.

Since 1996 that page had held a different picture, a fan that widened as it went. Stay with our reader at the table for a minute. I'd like to show you both pictures, and what each one is careful not to say.

The fan

The Bank first drew its inflation forecast as a fan in February 1996. A dark band runs down the middle, lighter bands lie on either side of it, and the whole shape spreads as it moves away from today.

The note under the chart told you how to read it, and it changed little over the years. Here it is as it stood in the report for August 2025. Imagine today's circumstances coming round a hundred times. In thirty of them inflation ends up in the dark band. In ninety it ends up somewhere inside the fan. In the other ten it lands outside, and the fan does not say where.

So the fan answers one question, how likely?, and it answers with a shape. What a shape cannot do is make you look at its edges. In April 2024 Ben Bernanke, asked by the Bank to review how it forecasts, reported that the fans rested on weak foundations, told a reader little that could not be said more directly, and got little attention from the public. His verdict was four words long.

They should be eliminated.

— Ben Bernanke, review for the Bank of England, April 2024

The three roads

The Bank did not go that far at once. In November 2025 its report kept a central forecast and set two scenarios beside it: one in which inflation proved stubborn, and one in which demand was weaker.

Then came the spring. The report our reader opened on 30 April 2026 was written after a conflict in the Middle East had sent energy prices up sharply. It set out three scenarios, which differed in the path of energy prices and in how far the rise spread into wages and other prices. It named none of them as central, and the fan was not in it.

A scenario answers a different question, what if? It tells a story with a cause in it: if energy stays dear, then this follows. It can describe something that has not happened before, which a fan drawn from past errors cannot. What it leaves out is the odds. Three roads on a page do not say which is likeliest, or whether the truth will run between them or beyond them all.

One forecast, three drawings

Here is one made-up forecast, drawn all three ways. Press the button and the box plays out a hundred futures, as the fan's note asks you to imagine, and marks where each one ends.

It names one number, 2.0.

It says 30 in 100 end in the middle band and 90 in 100 inside the fan.

three roads

It names three ways things could go, and gives no odds.

The forecast is invented and is not the Bank's. Each mark in the strip on the right is where one future ends, three years out.

Run it a few times. About five marks in a hundred land on the line, so the line is nearly always wrong, by a little or by a lot. About thirty land in the fan's middle band and about ninety inside the fan, as its note promised. And about four in ten land beyond the outer two roads, because the roads were never drawn as edges.

about this figure

The hundred futures are drawn from the fan's own arithmetic, so the fan is right here by construction. That is the assumption a fan makes: the future will miss in the way the past did. Without script the figure shows the three drawings and no marks.

What gets misread

Each drawing invites its own mistake. The line invites you to plan for one number. The fan invites you to take its edge for a wall. The roads, I suspect, invite you to pick one, most often the middle one, and call it the forecast.

The second mistake has been measured, on a different fan. American hurricane forecasts come with a cone, drawn so that the centre of the storm stays inside it about two times in three. Researchers at the University of Miami asked 2,847 people in Florida what the cone meant. Almost half took places outside it to be out of harm's way, and 40% thought it showed where the damage would be.

This year the forecasters are trying out a wider cone, meant to hold about 90% of outcomes, the same share as the Bank's fan. It will be wrong less often. I'd expect its edge to be read as a wall all the same.

November

Back to our reader. They chose a term in April, as you have to, with three roads in front of them and no odds.

If they open this month's report they will find the page changed again. The central line is back, and so is the fan around it, though the fan means something narrower now. In August 2025 it still leaned whichever way the Bank's committee judged the risks to lie. Now it is drawn only from the size of the Bank's own forecast errors since 2004, leaving out the pandemic, and the risks are told as two scenarios printed beside it.

So the page answers both questions at last. The fan says how far off forecasts like this have been before. The roads say what the Bank is worried about this time. It takes longer to read than one line did, and I think it is closer to what anybody knows.
