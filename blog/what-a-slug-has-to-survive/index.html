What a slug has to survive

17 November 2026

An editor finishes an article and gives it a title: Crème brûlée, à la carte. They press publish. The site turns the title into a web address, and the address comes out like this.

example.com/recipes/cr-me-br-l-e-la-carte

You have seen addresses like that one. Four letters went missing, the four with accents, and a hyphen is standing where each of them was. I'll show you the moment they were lost.

That readable tail of an address is called a slug. sluggi is the library I maintain for making them, and the rest of this page is what a title has to survive on the way.

Where the letters went

The editor's site did the obvious thing. It made the title lowercase and swapped everything that was not a to z for a hyphen. To that rule, è is simply something that is not a to z.

Here is the same title going through sluggi, one stage at a time. A shaded row is a stage that changed something. Put your own title in and watch.

An in-browser approximation of sluggi's default pipeline. The library has more stages: stopwords, truncation and your own replacement rules.

What else a title runs into

Back to our editor, who has a busy week ahead. Each of these arrives on their desk sooner or later.

Accents

This is the one that caught them on Monday. The fix is Unicode normalisation. In the form called NFKD, è becomes a plain e followed by a separate accent mark, and the mark can then be dropped. That gives creme-brulee.

Other alphabets

On Tuesday the title is in Russian. Normalisation does nothing for Привет, because a Cyrillic letter is not a Latin letter with something added. It needs a table that says П is P. sluggi has those tables for Cyrillic and Greek, so Привет, мир becomes privet-mir.

Text that arrives encoded

On Wednesday the title comes out of an older system with HTML entities still in it. Without a decoding step, Caf&eacute; becomes caf-eacute. With one it becomes cafe.

Punctuation at the edges

Swapping each unwanted character for a hyphen leaves doubled hyphens and stray ones at each end. Pulling the words out and joining them avoids both: --Hello, World!!-- becomes hello-world.

Length

Cutting a slug at a fixed number of characters can stop in the middle of a word. sluggi cuts at a word boundary by default, so The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog with a limit of 20 gives the-quick-brown-fox.

What sluggi leaves alone

I would rather you heard this from me than found it on a Friday. These are the default results from sluggi 0.1.2.

InputSlugWhy
Straßestraßeß has no accent to drop and no built-in mapping
ÆrøskøbingærøskøbingThe same is true of æ and ø
東京東京Only Greek and Cyrillic are transliterated
🎉 Party timeparty-timeEmoji are dropped by default. Turning them into their names needs the optional extra and a setting switched on
!!!an empty stringThere were no words

The first three still work in a web address, since browsers encode them, but they are not ASCII. If you need ASCII, pass your own mapping:

from sluggi import slugify

slugify("東京 station", custom_map={"東京": "tokyo"})   # 'tokyo-station'

The last row is the one to plan for. A title made only of punctuation or emoji gives an empty slug, and an empty slug is not an address. Check for it, and fall back to something that is always there, such as the record's id.

Monday, again

The editor gives an article a title: Crème brûlée, à la carte. They press publish. The address ends in creme-brulee-a-la-carte, and nobody notices anything, which is what a slug is for.
